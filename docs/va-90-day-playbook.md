# The VAI 90-Day Ownership Transfer Playbook

_The operating manual for a VAI-placed Virtual Assistant's first 90 days inside a
real estate team leader's business. It covers what happens day by day, what gets
measured, how to get a leader who will not slow down to hand over the expected
outcomes anyway, how AI gets layered in, and where this fails._

**Task:** [plans/tasks/20260804-va-90-day-playbook.md](../plans/tasks/20260804-va-90-day-playbook.md)

**Stays consistent with:** the six OQI dimensions and the mode routing in
[question-bank-export.md](question-bank-export.md), the quadrant read in
[reading-assessment-results.md](reading-assessment-results.md), and the story in
[narrative-source-of-truth.md](narrative-source-of-truth.md). If this doc and the
instrument ever disagree, the instrument wins and this doc gets corrected.

---

## 1. The job, in one sentence

**The VA is not an assistant. The VA is an ownership transfer agent.**

At day 90 the deliverable is not "tasks completed." It is: one workflow the
leader no longer touches, documented, staffed, measured, and proven by a retake
of the assessment.

That distinction is the whole playbook. Every failure mode in section 10 traces
back to a VA who quietly became a task-taker, and a leader who felt relief
without gaining freedom. Relief is not transfer. Relief ends when the VA takes a
vacation.

### Why 90 days is not an arbitrary number

The instrument already defines the finish line. Q018, the last question in every
workflow block:

> "Has your named owner successfully managed [Workflow] independently, with
> outcomes at or above your standard, for at least **90 days**?"

The assessment says a workflow is owned when someone else has run it at standard,
without the leader, for a quarter. So the placement is scoped to exactly that
window, and the retake at day 90 is the graduation exam the client already agreed
to when they took the baseline.

## 2. What we promise, and what we refuse to promise

Set this in writing at day 0. Most of the ways this engagement gets read as a
failure are avoidable expectation problems, not delivery problems.

**We promise at day 90:**

- One workflow fully transferred: documented, templated, with authority handed
  over and a backup trained.
- A measurable drop in the leader's hours on that workflow, tracked weekly from
  week 1 so nobody has to take it on faith.
- Movement on the per-workflow ODS and on five of the six OQI dimensions for that
  workflow.
- A deliverable pack the client owns outright (section 12).
- A second workflow started, with a signed Outcome Contract.

**We explicitly do not promise:**

- **A big move in the overall ODS.** Overall ODS averages all four workflows. We
  worked one. If the client expects the headline number to drop by 20 points in
  90 days, they will read a real success as a failure. Say this out loud on day
  0 and put it in the contract.
- **Confidence & Track Record (CT) at day 90.** CT is a lagging dimension by
  construction: Q018 needs 90 days of proven independent operation, and the VA
  does not reach independent operation until roughly day 60. At the day-90
  retake, expect DO, IE, SC, EC and OA to move and CT to trail. Predict it in
  advance and it reads as precision. Discover it at the retake and it reads as a
  miss. CT lands at the day-150 retake.
- **All four workflows.** One workflow fully transferred beats four half-done,
  every time. Half-transferred workflows come back.

## 3. Which workflow, and is this client even a fit

The four workflows are Listing Launch (C), Seller Communication (D), File
Opening (E), and Lender Tracking (F). The client's assessment already tells you
which one to attack, and whether a VA placement is the right product at all.

### The mode read

Each workflow is routed to a mode based on who the leader named as owner:

| Mode | Badge | What it means | VA fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Owner-Led | The leader personally owns it | **Ideal.** The VA becomes the first named owner. |
| **C** | Shared / No Clear Owner | It falls between roles or was never assigned | **Good.** The VA becomes the clear owner. |
| **B** | Team-Led | A TC, listing coordinator, or ops manager already owns it | **Do not start here.** |

**Never place a VA into a Mode B workflow.** Somebody already owns it. Inserting
a VA displaces a real person, creates a turf conflict, and the low OQI score you
were hired to fix is a coaching problem with the existing owner, not a staffing
gap. If the leader insists, the answer is "let's fix the ownership with the
person who has it, and I'll take a workflow nobody owns."

**Qualification signal for sales:** if all four workflows come back Mode B, this
client probably does not need a VA placement. They need their existing owners
coached. Selling them a VA sets up a failure that is nobody's fault.

### Picking the first workflow

1. Filter to Mode A and Mode C workflows only.
2. Among those, take the one with the highest **TBx1** answer (hours per week the
   leader personally spends on it). Highest hours means the biggest reclaim and,
   just as important, the most repetitions. You cannot build a track record on a
   workflow that runs twice a quarter.
3. Break ties with the leader's Section H urgency answers and their **TBx3** free
   text ("if you had to transfer this in the next 90 days, what's the biggest
   obstacle?").
4. Write the choice into the Outcome Contract, along with the workflow that is
   explicitly out of scope until day 76.

### Read TBx2 before the first conversation

TBx2 asks why the workflow has not been delegated. The answer changes the VA's
entire opening move:

| TBx2 answer | What is actually true | The VA's first move |
|---|---|---|
| "No one on my team has the skills yet" | Capability gap | Easiest win. Lead with what you have run before, propose shadow-then-run, ask for nothing but access. |
| "I haven't documented how it works" | Documentation gap | This is the product. "You will not write a single SOP. I will write them from watching you, and you correct them." |
| "I've tried to transfer it but it keeps coming back" | Authority problem, not capability | Hardest case. The takeback log and the Authority Transfer memo are the whole engagement. Escalate to the coach early; the VA cannot win this alone. |
| "I'm not sure I'm ready to let go of it" | Emotional, and honest | Do not push authority early. Extend the review ramp, agree the mistake protocol in week 1, and let the weekly numbers do the persuading. |
| "I haven't made it a priority yet" | No felt pain | Convert TBx1 hours into an annual number in the first session. Eight hours a week is a thousand hours a year. |

## 4. The six dimensions are the transfer sequence

The instrument does not just measure ownership, it defines it, in six dimensions
weighted by how much they matter. Read in the right order, they are a build
sequence. You cannot do them out of order.

| Order | Dimension | Weight | What has to exist | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Outcome Accountability (OA)** | 0.15 | The measurable standard, in the leader's words | Days 1 to 10 (set), days 76 to 90 (measured) |
| 2 | **Systems & Checklists (SC)** | 0.18 | SOP + templates + decision guide | Days 11 to 30 |
| 3 | **Escalation & Coverage (EC)** | 0.15 | Escalation ladder + trained backup | Days 31 to 55 |
| 4 | **Independent Execution (IE)** | 0.22 | The VA acts first, without asking | Days 56 to 75 |
| 5 | **Decision Ownership (DO)** | 0.20 | Formal authority, in writing, to the whole team | Days 56 to 75 |
| 6 | **Confidence & Track Record (CT)** | 0.10 | Time at standard | Day 90 onward |

The dependencies are real and they are the reason most delegation attempts fail:

- You cannot hand over authority (DO) before escalation thresholds exist (EC),
  or the leader is handing over a blank cheque and will pull it back the first
  time it is misused.
- You cannot write escalation thresholds (EC) before the SOP exists (SC),
  because a threshold is a statement about which step is unusual.
- You cannot write the SOP (SC) before you know what "good" means (OA), or you
  will document the steps and miss the standard. This is Q014 exactly: "not just
  did you do the steps but did it go well."

**So the first ten days are about OA, and nothing else.** That is the answer to
"how do we get the leader to slow down." We are not asking them to slow down for
everything. We are asking for one thing, once, at the start: what does good look
like and how will we know.

## 5. The Outcome Contract

One page per workflow. Six fields. This is the artifact everything else is built
backward from, and no VA proceeds past day 10 without it signed.

1. **The outcome, in one sentence.** What is true when this workflow has gone
   well. Written by the leader, in their words, not the VA's.
2. **The measurable standard.** Numbers with units and deadlines. "Listing live
   on MLS within 48 hours of signed agreement, photos ordered same day, seller
   receives a launch confirmation before end of day." Not "fast and
   professional." This is Q014, and it is the field leaders resist most.
3. **The three unacceptable failures.** The things that must never happen. These
   become the top of the escalation ladder and the alerts the VA builds first.
4. **Decision rights, in three tiers.** Tier 1: the VA decides alone. Tier 2: the
   VA decides and informs. Tier 3: the VA recommends and the leader decides.
   Every routine situation in the workflow gets assigned a tier. This is Q001 and
   Q010.
5. **The money and risk boundary.** The dollar figure or risk category above which
   anything becomes Tier 3, regardless of what the list says.
6. **The voice.** Can the VA send final communications to clients, vendors,
   lenders and title without review, and from which address, starting when. This
   is Q002, and it needs a date on it, not a "we'll see."

Plus one field the leader will not think to ask for:

7. **The mistake protocol.** What happens the first time the VA gets something
   wrong. Agreed in week 1, in writing, before there is any emotion attached to
   it. Section 10 explains why this single paragraph saves more engagements than
   anything else in the playbook.

## 6. How to get a leader who will not slow down to actually do this

Seven mechanics. They are not soft skills, they are procedures, and VAI trains
them before placement.

**1. The intake is already done. Use it.**
The leader answered 18 questions per workflow about exactly what they will not
let go of, plus TBx1 hours, TBx2 reasons, and a free-text obstacle in their own
words. The VA never opens with "tell me about your process." They open with
"you said the biggest obstacle to transferring Listing Launch is X, and you're
spending 4 to 8 hours a week on it. Here's what I think that means. Correct me."

**2. Ask for reactions, never for creation.**
This is the highest-leverage rule in the playbook. A leader who will not spend an
hour writing a process will spend ten minutes telling you your version is wrong.
So the VA always arrives with a draft: a draft contract, a draft SOP, a draft
template, a draft escalation tier. Being usefully wrong on paper beats being
politely blank. The VA's job is to be corrected, fast, on purpose.

**3. One long meeting, ever, and it is scheduled before day 1.**
Ninety minutes, week 1, the Outcome Contract session. Sell it as a trade: this is
the only long meeting you will be asked for in 90 days, everything after it is
fifteen minutes. Leaders will pay ninety minutes once for that deal. If it slips
twice, the coach escalates, not the VA (see failure mode 2).

**4. Open with the two-week question.**
Straight from the narrative doc: "if you disappeared for two weeks, what would
actually break?" It is the emotional centre of the whole product and it produces
better contract input in five minutes than an hour of process interviewing,
because it makes the leader think in outcomes instead of tasks.

**5. Batch every question. One list, one time, daily.**
A VA who pings fourteen times a day teaches a leader that delegation costs more
attention than doing it themselves, which is the exact belief we are trying to
break. Questions go on a running list and get asked once a day at a fixed time,
ordered by importance, with the VA's proposed answer next to each one so the
leader can just say "yes, yes, no, yes." This is a trainable behaviour and it is
non-negotiable.

**6. Short and frequent beats long and rare.**
Fifteen minutes daily in weeks 1 and 2, fifteen minutes three times a week in
weeks 3 to 6, fifteen minutes weekly after that. Fixed agenda every time, so it
cannot degrade into a task dump: numbers first, decisions needed second,
escalations third, and nothing else.

**7. Make the takebacks visible.**
Every time the leader reclaims something mid-flight, the VA logs it with the date
and the trigger. Nobody argues about it in the moment. It goes in the weekly
scorecard. The log is what turns "I feel like I'm still doing everything" into a
specific, fixable pattern, and it is the single best predictor of whether this
engagement will work.

## 7. The 90 days, day by day

Days 1 to 10 are specified daily because that is where engagements are won or
lost. After that the unit is the week, with a hard gate at the end of each phase.

### Phase 1: Contract (days 1 to 10)

**Day 1: Access and read-back.** Nothing is produced for the client yet.
- Access checklist completed: CRM login with the right permission level, email
  address on the client's domain, calendar, phone or dialer, transaction
  management system, shared drive, and any vendor portals (photography, MLS,
  sign install, title).
- The VA reads the client's full assessment results end to end, including the
  free-text answers.
- The VA writes a one-page read-back: "here is what I understand about how this
  business runs and where it runs through you." Sent same day.
- Fifteen-minute intro huddle. Introduce the daily question batch and the fixed
  huddle agenda.

**Day 2: The Outcome Contract session.** Ninety minutes, booked before day 1.
- Opens with the two-week question.
- Walks the six contract fields for the target workflow only.
- Recorded with consent and transcribed. The recording matters: the leader will
  say things in passing that turn out to be the standard.

**Day 3: Contract v1 drafted and sent.**
- Drafted from the transcript, deliberately specific, with the VA's best guesses
  filled in where the leader was vague.
- Sent with a deadline: "correct anything that's wrong by Friday, and I'll treat
  silence as agreement." Deadlines with a default are the only kind that work on
  a leader at this speed.

**Day 4: Shadowing starts.**
- The VA observes live transactions in the target workflow: sitting in, watching
  the inbox, reading the CRM activity, listening to recorded calls.
- Every step actually taken is logged in order, with who did it and how long it
  took. Not the idealised process. The real one.

**Day 5: Contract signed, baseline recorded.**
- Contract v2 agreed in the fifteen-minute huddle.
- Week 1 is the **baseline week** for Leader Touch Count (section 8). Measure it
  before anything changes, because this number is the headline for the next
  twelve weeks.

**Days 6 to 9: Capture the workflow as it is actually run.**
- Shadowing continues across as many live files as exist.
- The SOP is written as it goes, in the leader's real sequence including the
  parts that look inefficient. Efficiency comes later; fidelity comes first,
  because an SOP the leader does not recognise is an SOP they will not trust.
- Anything the VA does not understand goes on the daily batched question list
  with a proposed answer attached.

**Day 10: SOP v1 walkthrough. Phase gate.**
- Thirty minutes. The leader reacts to the document rather than producing it.
- The five highest-value open questions get asked, and only those.

> **Gate 1 (day 10):** Outcome Contract signed, including the measurable standard
> and the three decision-rights tiers. SOP v1 exists and the leader recognises it
> as how the work actually happens. Baseline Leader Touch Count recorded.
> **If the contract is not signed at day 10, stop and escalate to the coach.** Do
> not proceed into execution on a handshake. Every downstream artifact is derived
> from the contract, and building on an unsigned one guarantees rework and a
> disputed day-90 scorecard.

### Phase 2: Systems (days 11 to 30)

Building SC, the dimension worth 0.18.

- The VA executes steps of the workflow with the leader reviewing everything
  before it leaves the building.
- **Templates for every routine communication** (Q008 directly): seller weekly
  update, launch confirmation, vendor request, lender check-in, milestone notice,
  document request. Each one is drafted from the client's own past emails so it
  sounds like them, approved once by the leader, and then never reviewed again.
  That "approved once, then never again" mechanic is how review load falls
  without anyone feeling like they gave something up.
- **The decision guide begins** (Q009): every unusual situation encountered gets
  logged with what was decided and why. Twenty entries by day 55 is a decision
  guide, and it is the artifact that stops the phone ringing.
- Rework rate starts being tracked from day 11 (section 8). It is the only honest
  measure of whether the stated standard is the real standard.

> **Gate 2 (day 30):** SOP coverage at 100% of the steps observed. Every routine
> communication templated and approved. Decision guide has entries. Rework rate
> measured and trending down. **AI setup does not begin before this gate**
> (section 9).

### Phase 3: Escalation and coverage (days 31 to 55)

Building EC, and this is the phase most delegation attempts skip entirely.

- The VA runs the workflow end to end. The leader's review moves from **before**
  to **after**, and then thins on a fixed ramp: 100% of files reviewed in week 5,
  50% in week 6, 25% in week 7, spot checks in week 8. Publishing the ramp in
  advance is what stops it from becoming a negotiation every week.
- **The escalation ladder gets written from real escalations, not imagined ones.**
  Every escalation in the week gets classified Tier 1, 2, or 3 in the weekly
  review. Anything the VA escalated that was actually Tier 1 is an
  unnecessary escalation (Q011) and the reason gets noted. Anything the VA
  handled that should have been Tier 3 updates the contract.
- **Coverage gets built** (Q012): a named backup who knows the escalation
  thresholds well enough to keep the workflow moving for a full day. The backup
  can be the team's TC, another VAI VA, or a cross-trained team member. This is a
  scored dimension and it is routinely ignored, which is how a delegation win
  turns into a new single point of failure.

> **Gate 3 (day 55):** Unnecessary escalation rate under 10%. Takeback count at
> zero for two consecutive weeks. Backup named and trained, with one live
> half-day handled by the backup as proof. **Do not transfer authority until
> takebacks are at zero.** Authority handed to someone the leader is still
> quietly overruling is theatre, and the retake will show it.

### Phase 4: Authority transfer (days 56 to 75)

Building DO and IE, together worth 0.42, the heaviest phase in the instrument.

- **The Authority Transfer memo.** Written, dated, and sent by the leader to the
  entire team, not just to the VA. It names the VA as the owner of the workflow,
  states the three tiers, and tells the team where to route things. Without it,
  the team keeps emailing the leader out of habit and every metric on the VA's
  side can look perfect while nothing actually changes for the leader.
- **The VA sends final communications without review** (Q002), from the date in
  contract field 6.
- **Physically remove the leader from the routing.** This is the most
  under-appreciated step in the entire playbook: unsubscribe the leader from
  workflow-stage notifications in the CRM, take them off the transaction email
  threads, reassign the CRM task owner, update the shared inbox rules, change who
  the vendor portals notify. A leader who still receives the alert will still act
  on the alert. Announcements do not change behaviour; routing does.
- **The mistake protocol goes live** (Q003 and Q006). When the VA gets something
  wrong, the VA owns the resolution and reports the fix, not the problem. The
  format is fixed: what happened, what I did about it, what I changed so it does
  not recur. Never "what should I do?"

> **Gate 4 (day 75):** Leader Touch Count at or below the contract target. Zero
> pre-send reviews for two consecutive weeks. Authority memo sent to the team.
> Leader removed from all routine notification paths, verified by opening their
> inbox and CRM settings rather than by asking.

### Phase 5: Proof and extension (days 76 to 90)

- **The scorecard**: performance against the contract's measurable standard for
  the full 90 days, with the leader's hours plotted week by week from the
  baseline.
- **The retake**: the coach generates a tokenized retake link from the admin
  session page and the leader retakes the assessment. The delta view shows
  movement against baseline, per workflow and per dimension. This is the
  mechanism built in the
  [coaching pilot](../plans/tasks/20260719-coaching-poc-pilot.md).
- **The two-week test, in miniature.** The leader goes dark on the target
  workflow for three consecutive business days: no notifications, no approvals,
  no check-ins. The VA runs it. Everything that breaks gets logged and fixed. A
  clean three-day test is the story the client tells other team leaders, and it
  is worth more to VAI than the scorecard.
- **Workflow 2 kickoff**: a new Outcome Contract session for the next workflow,
  same six fields. It moves faster because the method is established and the
  leader now believes it works. Target 45 days for the second, 30 for the third.

> **Gate 5 (day 90):** Deliverable pack handed over (section 12). Retake taken
> and the delta reviewed with the coach and the client together. Workflow 2
> contract signed. Three-day dark test completed with the failures logged.

## 8. How we know if we are making progress

Three tiers, because the instrument is a quarterly measure and an engagement that
waits 90 days to find out whether it is working has already failed.

### Tier 1: Weekly operational metrics

Tracked by the VA, reviewed with the leader every Friday. These move long before
scores do.

| Metric | Definition | Target trajectory |
|---|---|---|
| **Leader Touch Count** | Times the leader touched the workflow this week: decisions made, comms reviewed, escalations answered, tasks done personally | Falls every week from the week 1 baseline. **The headline number.** |
| **Takeback Count** | Times the leader reclaimed something mid-flight | Zero by day 55, and stays there |
| **Unnecessary Escalation Rate** | Escalations the VA could have handled under the contract, divided by total escalations (Q011) | Rises weeks 3 to 6, then falls below 10% by day 55 |
| **Rework Rate** | Percentage of VA output the leader edited before it went out | Below 10% by day 55, below 5% by day 75 |
| **SOP Coverage** | Percentage of observed workflow steps with a written SOP step | 100% by day 30 |
| **Standard Adherence** | Performance against the contract's measurable standard | At standard by day 55, above by day 75 |
| **Unplanned Work Share** | Percentage of the VA's week spent on ad hoc requests outside the target workflow | Under 20% in month 1, under 10% after |

**If you only look at one number, look at Leader Touch Count.** If it is not
falling by week 6, the engagement is off track no matter how good the
relationship feels, how much the leader likes the VA, or how much work is getting
done. Everything else on this list exists to explain why it is not falling.

Two of these deserve a note. **Unnecessary escalation rate is supposed to rise
first**, because a VA who escalates nothing in week 3 is a VA who is guessing.
And **rework rate is the lie detector**: a leader who signed off on "48 hours to
MLS" but edits 40% of what goes out has a real standard they have not told you.
That gap is a day-30 conversation, not a day-90 surprise.

### Tier 2: The instrument

Baseline at day 0 (their pre-sale assessment), retake at day 90.

- **Per-workflow ODS on the target workflow.** This is the number that judges the
  engagement.
- **The six OQI dimensions individually**, in the expected order: SC moves first,
  then EC, then DO and IE together, then OA. CT lags past day 90 by design.
- **Overall ODS and DRS**, read as context only.

Two measurement rules, both of which follow from the master plan's
"scores are a measurement instrument" principle:

1. **Judge the engagement on per-workflow ODS, not overall ODS.** Overall ODS is
   an average across four workflows and we worked one. Agreed at day 0, in
   writing, in the contract.
2. **An optional day-45 mid-check is a manual dimension read, not a retake.** Do
   not run a full retake at 45 days just to feel progress. It burns the
   comparability that makes the day-90 number persuasive, and the Tier 1 metrics
   already tell you everything a mid-check would.

### Tier 3: Business outcome

Asked identically at day 0 and day 90, so the answers are comparable.

- **Hours per week the leader spends on this workflow.** The day-0 value is
  already captured: it is their TBx1 answer.
- **Did the leader take time away without the workflow stalling?** Binary, and it
  is the narrative doc's "vacation that wasn't" answered in the affirmative.
- **The two-week question, answered concretely.** At day 0 they could not name
  what would break. At day 90 they can name what would not.

### The weekly scorecard

One page, same shape every Friday, sent whether or not the huddle happens: the
seven Tier 1 metrics with their trend arrows, the takeback log for the week,
decisions needed from the leader, and the current phase gate with days remaining.
Consistency is the point. Twelve of these in a row is the evidence base for the
renewal conversation, and it means the day-90 result is never a surprise to
anybody.

## 9. Where AI fits, and where it does not

Two guardrails first, because both failure modes here are expensive.

**Guardrail 1: no AI before Gate 2 (day 30).** You cannot automate a workflow you
have not documented. Automating an undocumented process makes the mess faster and
harder to inspect, and it hands the leader a black box at exactly the moment they
are deciding whether to trust the VA. A VA who shows up in week 1 excited about
building a bot is a VA about to lose the engagement.

**Guardrail 2: nothing reaches a client in the leader's voice until the template
is approved.** This leader has already been burned by delegation. One AI-drafted
email that sounds wrong going to a seller costs more trust than three weeks of
good work buys.

### Tier 1: Capture and drafting (days 11 to 30, zero setup)

This tier alone justifies the AI layer, because it is the direct answer to "the
leader will not slow down."

- **Transcribe the Outcome Contract session and the shadowing sessions, then turn
  the transcripts into SOP drafts and contract drafts.** The leader talks, which
  they will do; the documentation appears, which they will not do. This converts
  the one behaviour leaders reliably offer into the artifact the engagement needs.
- **Extract the leader's voice from their own sent mail.** Pull the last twenty
  real seller updates or lender emails, and generate templates that sound like
  them rather than like a template. Approve once.
- **Order and compress the daily question batch**, with a proposed answer next to
  each question so the leader is confirming rather than composing.
- **Draft decision guide entries** from the escalation log at the end of each week.

### Tier 2: Skills (days 31 to 60)

A "skill" here means a named, saved, reusable instruction set the VA invokes the
same way every time: a Claude Project or a custom GPT loaded with the SOP, the
Outcome Contract, the approved templates, and the voice guide.

**The key insight: the SOP and the AI skill are the same artifact.** The SOP,
written properly, is the skill's instructions. That collapses two pieces of work
into one and it is why documentation quality compounds instead of rotting.

Name skills after workflow steps, not after capabilities:

- **Draft seller weekly update.** Inputs: transaction status, last update sent.
  Output: a draft in the leader's approved voice.
- **Listing launch readiness check.** Input: the file. Output: what is missing
  against the SOP checklist, in checklist order.
- **Escalation classifier.** Input: the situation. Output: Tier 1, 2, or 3 with
  the contract clause quoted. This one is worth building first: it makes the
  escalation ladder self-enforcing, it directly attacks the unnecessary
  escalation rate, and it teaches the VA the contract by using it.
- **File completeness audit.** Input: the file contents. Output: gaps against the
  document checklist.
- **Weekly scorecard assembly.** Inputs: the logs. Output: the Friday one-pager.

### Tier 3: Connections and workflows (days 60 to 90, only if Tier 2 is stable)

**Connections** get added in order of risk: read-only CRM access first, so the AI
can see transaction status; then calendar; then the shared inbox; then document
storage; then write access to the CRM, last.

**Workflows** are triggered automations, and each one gets built only after the
manual version has run reliably for two weeks:

- Transaction reaches "listing agreement signed" in the CRM, so the file-opening
  checklist is created and assigned to the VA.
- Day 5 with no MLS live, so the VA gets alerted. **The VA, not the leader.**
  Every alert that still routes to the leader is a Leader Touch Count point and
  quietly undoes Phase 4.
- Monday 7am, so the seller weekly updates are drafted into the VA's review queue.
- Lender has not responded in 72 hours, so a follow-up is drafted.

**Hard rule: every automation has a named human owner and a visible log.** Nothing
sends to a client unreviewed unless the template is approved and the trigger is
deterministic. An automation nobody can explain is a liability the day the VA
leaves.

### The recommended stack (open decision, see section 13)

- **AI layer: Claude**, with a Project per client holding the contract, SOP,
  templates, and voice guide. Projects give VAI a portable skill format that can
  be templated across placements, which is where the compounding in section 11
  comes from.
- **System of record: whatever CRM the client already has.** Follow Up Boss,
  Sisu, kvCORE, Lofty, whichever. **Never migrate a client's CRM during the first
  90 days.** It is a separate project, it will consume the engagement, and it
  will get blamed for every problem that occurs during it.
- **Document home: the client's Google Drive**, not the VA's and not VAI's. The
  deliverable pack has to be the client's property from day 1, both because it is
  the honest arrangement and because it is what makes VA turnover survivable.
- **Automation layer: the CRM's native automation first**, and a general
  automation tool only when the CRM genuinely cannot do it. Every tool added is a
  thing the next VA has to learn.

## 10. Where this fails

Ranked by how often it will happen, with the detection signal and the
countermeasure. Every one of these is preventable and none of them are
theoretical.

**1. The VA becomes a task-taker.** The leader dumps ad hoc work, the VA is
genuinely helpful, everyone feels good, and nothing transfers. At day 90 the
client says "she's great, but I'm still working the same hours."
*Signal:* SOP coverage flat past day 30, Leader Touch Count not falling, unplanned
work share above 30%.
*Counter:* the unplanned work cap as a tracked metric, the contract naming one
workflow explicitly, and the Friday scorecard showing the split every single week.

**2. The contract never gets signed because the leader will not sit down.**
*Signal:* still on contract v1 at day 10.
*Counter:* the session is booked before day 1 as a condition of engagement start.
If it slips twice, **the coach escalates to the leader, never the VA.** A VA
pressuring a team leader is a power mismatch: the VA will lose, and then stop
trying, and the engagement is dead by day 30 without anyone naming why.

**3. The takeback.** The first time something goes wrong, the leader reclaims the
workflow, and the transfer resets to zero.
*Signal:* takeback count above zero after day 45.
*Counter:* the mistake protocol, agreed in writing in week 1, before there is any
emotion attached to it. Say it plainly on day 2: "we should decide now what
happens the first time I get something wrong, because there will be a first
time." Pre-negotiated errors are survivable; surprise errors are not.

**4. The team keeps routing to the leader.** Authority was transferred on paper,
but the TC, the agents, the lender and the title rep all still email the leader
because they always have.
*Signal:* every VA-side metric looks excellent while Leader Touch Count stays
flat. This combination means routing, every time.
*Counter:* the authority memo goes to the whole team, and Phase 4's physical
routing changes get verified in the leader's actual inbox and CRM settings, not
confirmed by asking.

**5. AI arrives too early.** An automation exists before the SOP does. Nobody can
explain what it does, and the leader's trust drops at exactly the wrong moment.
*Signal:* any AI build before Gate 2.
*Counter:* Gate 2 is a hard gate.

**6. The VA can execute but cannot facilitate.** Getting outcomes out of a
resistant, busy, slightly defensive leader is a consulting skill, not an admin
skill. It is the single biggest predictor of VA success and it is not what VAs
are usually hired for.
*Signal:* contract sessions run long and produce vague fields, especially field 2.
*Counter:* VAI trains and role-plays the contract session before placement, and
**the coach attends or reviews the recording of every new VA's first three
contract sessions.** This is a VAI selection and training problem, not a client
problem, and it must not be discovered in the field.

**7. Measuring the wrong number at day 90.** Overall ODS moved four points
because we worked one of four workflows, and the client reads a real success as a
failure. Similarly, CT did not move and the client concludes the VA is not
trusted.
*Signal:* entirely predictable, which is what makes it inexcusable.
*Counter:* section 2, in writing, at day 0.

**8. The VA becomes the new bottleneck.** The work moved off the leader and onto
one person with no backup. The client has not gained resilience, they have
changed who they are dependent on.
*Signal:* Q012 unmoved at retake.
*Counter:* coverage is a Phase 3 deliverable with a live half-day proof, not an
afterthought.

**9. VA turnover at month 5.** The VA leaves and the knowledge walks out with them.
*Signal:* the deliverable pack lives in the VA's tools or VAI's, not the client's.
*Counter:* everything lives in the client's Drive from day 1. Done properly this
inverts into the strongest thing VAI sells: a replacement VA is productive in
days rather than months, because the SOP, the skills and the templates are all
still there. Say that out loud during the sale.

**10. Scope creep across workflows.** The leader wants all four now.
*Signal:* obvious, and it usually shows up around day 40 when the first workflow
starts working.
*Counter:* the contract names the one workflow and the date the second starts.
"Yes, on day 76" is a much better answer than "no."

**11. The stated standard is not the real standard.** The leader signed off on a
number but is actually rejecting anything that is not exactly how they would have
done it.
*Signal:* rework rate above 20% while the scorecard shows the standard being met.
*Counter:* rework rate is tracked from day 11 precisely to surface this, and the
gap is a scheduled day-30 conversation.

**12. Attribution loss.** At day 90 the client credits the VA rather than VAI's
system, and concludes they can hire the VA directly or find a cheaper one.
*Signal:* business-level, and it ties directly to the
[business operations track](../plans/tasks/20260720-business-operations-track.md).
*Counter:* the retake delta and the deliverable pack are VAI-branded and delivered
by the coach, so the method is visibly the product and the VA is visibly the
operator. The contractual side (non-solicit) is a B0 legal item, not a playbook
item.

## 11. How this becomes a massive success

- **One workflow, fully transferred, beats four half-done.** Sell it that way and
  hold the line when the leader pushes at day 40.
- **The retake delta is the renewal asset.** A client who sees a number move
  renews and refers. That is the coaching pilot's success bar, applied to a
  service line.
- **Put Leader Touch Count in front of the leader every Friday.** The felt
  experience of relief is vague and easy to forget. A falling number, twelve
  weeks in a row, is not.
- **Schedule the first mistake.** Trust survives an error that was pre-negotiated
  and rarely survives one that was not.
- **Make the three-day dark test the graduation ceremony.** The assessment's most
  emotionally loaded question was "what breaks if you disappear." Answering it out
  loud, with evidence, is the referral engine.
- **Compounding is the actual business.** Workflow 2 takes 45 days, workflow 3
  takes 30, because the method is established and the client believes. All four
  workflows transfer inside nine months, and *then* the overall ODS moves for
  real.
- **Standardise across placements, and VAI gets faster every time.** The Listing
  Launch SOP built for client A is 80% of the SOP for client B. By placement ten
  a VA arrives on day 1 with a draft SOP, a template library, and five working
  skills that need client-specific correction rather than invention. The 90 days
  compresses toward 60, margin improves, and quality stops depending on which VA
  you got.

That last point is the strategic one. **This playbook is not just service
delivery, it is the asset that makes L3 licensing credible.** A brokerage will not
license a coach's intuition. They will license a reproducible system with an
instrument attached that proves it worked, which is exactly what B3 in the
[business operations track](../plans/tasks/20260720-business-operations-track.md)
is waiting on.

## 12. The day-90 deliverable pack

Handed over as the client's property, in the client's Drive:

1. The signed Outcome Contract for workflow 1, plus the newly signed one for
   workflow 2.
2. The SOP, current as run, not as originally drafted.
3. The template library, every routine communication, approved.
4. The decision guide, built from real situations encountered.
5. The escalation ladder with the three tiers and the named backup.
6. The Authority Transfer memo as sent to the team.
7. Twelve weekly scorecards.
8. The day-90 scorecard against the measurable standard.
9. The retake delta, per workflow and per dimension, with the coach's read.
10. The three-day dark test log: what held, what broke, what was fixed.
11. The AI skill definitions, exportable and documented in plain language.
